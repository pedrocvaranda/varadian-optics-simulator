00 / STARTING POINT

The Question That Started It All

CONTEXT · MOTIVATION
the gap

Classical geometric optics — Snell's law, Fermat's principle, reflection — was built on an implicit assumption: space is flat. Nobody had systematically asked: what happens to these laws if space itself is curved?

the origin

This paper was born from a high-school curiosity question: "Is there a non-Euclidean geometric optics?" The teacher said no, and challenged the student to build it. This is the result.

why it matters

Metamaterials can engineer refractive index profiles that emulate curved geometries physically.
General Relativity treats gravity as spacetime curvature — light bends because it follows geodesics.
Transformational optics already exploits coordinate changes; Varandian Optics gives it an intrinsic geometric foundation.

01 / KEY CONCEPT

Non-Euclidean Geometry

WHAT CHANGES WHEN K ≠ 0
intuition

Euclidean geometry (K = 0): parallel lines never meet. But on a sphere, two meridians start parallel at the equator and converge at the poles. On a saddle surface, they diverge forever. Curvature K is the single number that captures this.

parallels converge
parallels stay parallel
parallels diverge
the curvature parameter
K > 0 → spherical geometry (sphere of radius R = 1/√K)
K = 0 → Euclidean plane
K < 0 → hyperbolic geometry (Poincaré disk model)
02 / KEY CONCEPT

Geodesics

THE GENERALIZED "STRAIGHT LINE"
intuition

A geodesic is the shortest path between two points on a surface. On the plane, it is a straight line. On a sphere, it is an arc of a great circle — like the routes that airplanes actually fly. On the Poincaré disk, geodesics are arcs of circles that meet the boundary at right angles.

the metric — Eq. (1)

Everything follows from how distances are measured. In polar geodesic coordinates:

ds² = dr² + SK(r)² dθ²

// S_K(r) is the "angular stretch factor" of the space:
S_K(r) = (1/√K) · sin(√K · r) // K > 0
S_K(r) = r // K = 0 (recovers Euclidean)
S_K(r) = (1/√|K|) · sinh(√|K| · r) // K < 0

On the plane, the "ring" at radius r has circumference 2πr. On a sphere it is smaller. On a hyperbolic space it is larger. S_K encodes exactly this.

03 / PHYSICAL FOUNDATION

Fermat's Principle

WHY LIGHT FOLLOWS GEODESICS
intuition

Fermat (1662) observed that light always takes the path of least optical time — not necessarily the shortest geometric path. This is why light bends when entering water: it finds the route that minimizes total travel time.

from principle to geodesic
// Optical time:
T = ∫ n(r)/c · ds

// Fermat: δT = 0 (light extremizes optical time)

// Define a new metric: g_ij = n(r)² δ_ij
// Then: T = (1/c) ∫ n(r) ds = (1/c) ∫ ds_g

δ ∫ n(r) ds_g = 0

// This is exactly: minimize arc-length in metric g
// = follow a geodesic of g ✓
the key insight

A medium with refractive index n(r) is a curved metric in disguise. Fermat's principle, applied in a space of constant curvature K, forces light to follow geodesics of that space. This is not an assumption — it is a theorem.

04 / CORE DERIVATION

The Ray Equation

EQUATION (3) OF THE PAPER
intuition

We want the curve r(θ) that a light ray traces in a space of curvature K. The tool is the same Lagrangian mechanics used in classical physics — but applied to optics.

step-by-step derivation
// Lagrangian = arc-length element squared:
L = ṙ² + S_K(r)² · θ̇²

// θ is cyclic (doesn't appear explicitly) → conserved momentum:
p_θ = ∂L/∂θ̇ = 2 S_K(r)² · θ̇ = const ≡ 2C

// Energy E = L = 1 (arc-length parametrization):
ṙ² + S_K(r)² · θ̇² = 1

// Substitute θ̇ = C / S_K(r)²:
ṙ² = 1 − C² / S_K(r)²

// Divide through: (dr/dθ)² = ṙ²/θ̇²
(dr/dθ)² = S_K(r)²/C² · [ S_K(r)² − C² ] ← Eq. (3)
C is the impact parameter — the optical analogue of angular momentum. It measures how oblique the ray is with respect to the center. Every ray in every curvature is described by this single equation, parametrized by C and K.
05 / EXPLICIT SOLUTIONS

Geodesics in Closed Form

EQUATIONS (4) AND (5)
sphere — K = +1
S_K(r) = sin(r)

// Solving Eq. (3) with S = sin r:
tan r(θ) = − c / [ A cos(θ − θ₀) ] ← Eq. (4)
hyperbolic — K = −1 (Poincaré disk)
S_K(r) = sinh(r)

tanh r(θ) = − α / [ B cos(θ − θ₀) ] ← Eq. (5)
geometric meaning

Sphere (K > 0): Eq. (4) describes great-circle arcs — the analog of straight lines on the globe.

Hyperbolic (K < 0): Eq. (5) describes circular arcs that meet the boundary of the Poincaré disk at right angles. This is precisely the definition of hyperbolic geodesics — the algebra confirms the geometry.

the verification

Solving the differential equation independently and recovering the known geodesics of each geometry is the internal consistency check of the framework. The math works.

06 / LAW I

Geodesic Propagation

FIRST VARANDIAN LAW
intuition

The classical law "light travels in straight lines" is replaced by its natural generalization: in a Varandian medium, light travels along the geodesic of curvature K.

formal statement
K = 0 → straight lines // classical Euclidean optics
K > 0 → great-circle arcs // spherical optics
K < 0 → hyperbolic lines // Poincaré disk geodesics

Classical optics recovered: Setting K = 0 gives S_K(r) = r, the Euclidean metric. Every Varandian law reduces to its classical counterpart. Classical geometric optics is the K = 0 special case of Varandian Optics.

07 / INTERACTIVE

The Poincaré Disk

K = −1 · CLICK TO DRAW GEODESICS
what you're looking at

The Poincaré disk is a model of the entire hyperbolic plane compressed into a unit circle. Straight lines in this geometry are arcs of circles that meet the boundary at 90°. Click inside the disk to place two points — the geodesic between them appears instantly.

click to place points · click a third time to reset
why the arcs are "straight"

In the Poincaré disk metric ds² = 4(dx² + dy²)/(1−r²)², points near the boundary are "infinitely far" from the center. The circular arcs are the paths that minimize this hyperbolic distance — they are geodesics, not curves.

08 / LAW II · ORIGINAL CONTRIBUTION

Varandian Refraction

EQUATION (6) — GENERALIZED SNELL'S LAW
intuition

Snell's law — n₁sinθ₁ = n₂sinθ₂ — comes from conservation of tangential momentum at an interface. In curved spaces, the "angular lever arm" is no longer simply r but S_K(r). When the two regions have different curvatures K₁ and K₂, the interface joins two distinct geometries.

the generalization
// Classical Snell (K = 0 on both sides):
n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

// Varandian Snell — Eq. (6):
n₁ S_{K₁}(r) sin θ₁ = n₂ S_{K₂}(r) sin θ₂

// Setting K₁ = K₂ = 0: S_K(r) = r cancels → classical law recovered ✓
⚡ This is the genuinely novel equation of the paper. The case K₁ ≠ K₂ — refraction at the interface between two regions of different constant curvature — had not been formalized in the literature the author surveyed.
physical picture

Imagine a lens where the interior has been engineered to behave as a hyperbolic space (K < 0) and the exterior is flat (K = 0). Eq. (6) governs exactly how light bends entering that lens — a formula classical optics cannot provide.

09 / LAW IV

Index–Curvature Duality

EQUATION (7) · BRIDGE TO PHYSICAL REALITY
intuition

A medium with spatially varying refractive index n(ρ) distorts light paths. The central insight: you can choose n(ρ) so that light behaves exactly as if it were in a space of constant curvature K. Curved geometry becomes a manufacturable material.

the exact profiles — Eq. (7)
// To emulate K = +1 (spherical) in Euclidean space:
n(ρ) = 2 / (1 + ρ²)

// To emulate K = −1 (hyperbolic) in Euclidean space:
n(ρ) = 2 / (1 − ρ²), ρ < 1
connection to metamaterials

The hyperbolic profile n(ρ) = 2/(1−ρ²) diverges at ρ → 1, meaning the effective "optical distance" to the boundary is infinite — consistent with the Poincaré disk where the boundary is infinitely far. A physical metamaterial approximating this profile would make light behave as if space were hyperbolic inside the lens.

10 / ORIGINALITY

What Is New Here?

CONTEXT · CONTRIBUTION · LIMITATIONS
honest assessment

The ingredients — Fermat's principle, Riemannian geometry, the Poincaré disk — were not invented here. What is new is the systematic synthesis: taking all of geometric optics and rederiving it on a substrate of constant curvature K, including the interface law between different curvatures.

three levels of contribution

1. Reformulation: Fermat + Riemannian geometry → covariant optical laws valid for any K.
2. Derivation: Explicit closed-form geodesics r(θ) for K = ±1, verified against known geometry.
3. New law: Eq. (6) — refraction at the interface between regions of distinct constant curvature K₁ ≠ K₂.

limitations (from the paper)

· Geometric optics is valid only in the short-wavelength limit. Diffraction and interference are outside this framework.
· Extending Fermat to K ≠ 0 is a conceptual extrapolation, not a derivation from a wave equation.
· The index–curvature duality (Eq. 7) is a novel synthesis, not a direct theorem in Luneburg's work.
· Following Lakatos: this is a conjecture open to validation, criticism, and refinement.

Geometric Optics ⊂ Varandian Optics
